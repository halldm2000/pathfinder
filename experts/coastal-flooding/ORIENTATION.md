# Coastal Flooding Expert Orientation

Reference material for the coastal flooding expert. Facts here correct what the model gets wrong, add what it doesn't know, and anchor fast-moving details with dates. Omits facts the model reliably knows (basic fluid dynamics, gravity waves, tidal forcing, etc.).

Last verified: March 2026. Claude's training data extends through early 2025.

## Field Map

Coastal flooding prediction sits at the intersection of atmospheric forcing, ocean dynamics, and land topography. The field has matured operationally -- NOAA runs SLOSH for real-time surge warnings, FEMA uses ADCIRC for regulatory flood mapping -- but compound flooding (surge + rain + river) remains an active research frontier with no operational joint-probability system deployed as of March 2026. Sea level rise is accelerating the baseline: IPCC AR6 projects 0.28-1.01 m of global mean sea level rise by 2100 depending on emissions pathway, with some regions (U.S. Gulf Coast, Southeast Asia) facing significantly higher local rates due to subsidence and ocean dynamics. AI/ML surrogates for surge models are a growing research area but have not entered operational forecasting. The convergence point is total water level forecasting: combining surge, tide, wave setup, sea level rise, and precipitation into a single actionable inundation estimate.

## Storm Surge Physics and Models

Storm surge is the abnormal rise of water generated by a storm's winds, pressure, and motion. Wind stress is the dominant driver (~90% of surge in most cases); the inverted barometer effect from low pressure contributes ~1 cm per hPa deficit. Surge magnitude depends on: wind speed, storm size (wind radii), forward speed, angle of approach to the coastline, and nearshore bathymetry. Wide, shallow continental shelves (Gulf of Mexico) amplify surge; narrow, steep shelves (U.S. Northeast) produce less amplification but more wave action.

### SLOSH (Sea, Lake, and Overland Surges from Hurricanes)
- **Developer:** NWS/NHC. Operational since 1985, continuously updated.
- **Physics:** 2D depth-averaged, linearized shallow water equations with parameterized bottom friction. No wave coupling, no rainfall.
- **Resolution:** Variable -- polar grids centered on coastline features, typically 100 m to 5 km. 35+ operational basins covering U.S. coastline.
- **Operational role:** Primary NWS operational surge forecasting tool. P-Surge (probabilistic SLOSH) runs thousands of ensemble members using TC track/intensity uncertainty. MOMs (Maximum of Maximums) and MEOWs (Maximum Envelopes of Water) provide planning products.
- **Strengths:** Fast execution, decades of calibration, institutional trust.
- **Limitations:** No wave setup, no rainfall, no riverine interaction, simplified physics. Adequate for hurricane surge warnings but insufficient for compound flood assessment.

### ADCIRC (Advanced Circulation Model)
- **Developer:** University of North Carolina, University of Notre Dame, others. Open source.
- **Physics:** 2D/3D unstructured-grid finite element model solving the shallow water equations. Coupled to SWAN (Simulated WAves Nearshore) for wave-current interaction. Wetting/drying for inundation.
- **Resolution:** Unstructured triangular mesh -- 20-50 m in coastal zones, 50+ km in open ocean. Single mesh spans ocean to floodplain.
- **Operational role:** FEMA regulatory flood mapping (coastal flood insurance studies). CERA (Coastal Emergency Risks Assessment) portal provides real-time ADCIRC guidance during hurricanes. Used by USACE for coastal engineering studies. Not the primary NWS warning tool (SLOSH fills that role).
- **Strengths:** High resolution where needed, wave coupling, physics fidelity, large community, extensive validation.
- **Limitations:** Computationally expensive (HPC required), complex mesh generation, long setup time for new domains.

### SCHISM (Semi-implicit Cross-scale Hydroscience Integrated System Model)
- **Developer:** VIMS (Virginia Institute of Marine Science). Open source.
- **Physics:** 3D unstructured-grid model using semi-implicit time stepping. Baroclinic, wave-coupled (WWM), includes sediment transport and water quality modules.
- **Resolution:** Unstructured, similar flexibility to ADCIRC. Particularly strong for estuarine and compound flooding applications.
- **Operational role:** NOAA OCS operational nowcast/forecast systems (e.g., STOFS -- Surge and Tide Operational Forecast System). Growing adoption for compound flood research.
- **Strengths:** Semi-implicit numerics allow larger time steps (faster than ADCIRC for comparable resolution), 3D baroclinic capability, compound flood readiness.
- **Limitations:** Smaller user community than ADCIRC, fewer published validation studies, less FEMA regulatory history.

See `reference/surge-models.md` for detailed architecture, input requirements, and operational configuration.

## Sea Level Rise

### IPCC AR6 Projections (2021)
Global mean sea level rise by 2100 relative to 1995-2014 baseline:
- **SSP1-2.6 (low emissions):** 0.32-0.62 m (likely range), central estimate ~0.44 m
- **SSP2-4.5 (intermediate):** 0.44-0.76 m, central estimate ~0.56 m
- **SSP5-8.5 (high emissions):** 0.63-1.01 m, central estimate ~0.77 m
- **Low-confidence / ice sheet instability:** Cannot exclude 2 m by 2100 under high emissions if marine ice cliff instability occurs. AR6 treats this as low confidence but not negligible.

### Regional Variation
Sea level change is not uniform. Key drivers of regional departure from global mean:
- **Glacial isostatic adjustment (GIA):** Land still rebounding from last ice age. Scandinavia rising (relative sea level falling), U.S. East Coast subsiding (relative sea level rising faster than global mean).
- **Ocean dynamics:** Changes in currents and heat distribution. Gulf Stream slowdown could raise sea level on U.S. Northeast coast by an additional 0.1-0.3 m.
- **Land subsidence:** Groundwater extraction, sediment compaction. Mississippi Delta, Jakarta, Bangkok, parts of Houston experience 5-25 mm/yr subsidence on top of sea level rise.
- **Gravitational fingerprinting:** Melting ice sheets reduce local gravitational pull, redistributing water globally. Greenland melt raises sea level more in the Southern Hemisphere; Antarctic melt raises it more in the Northern Hemisphere.

### Observed Rates
- **Global mean (satellite altimetry, 1993-present):** ~3.6 mm/yr, accelerating at ~0.08 mm/yr^2.
- **Tide gauge long-term (20th century):** ~1.5 mm/yr globally, accelerating since ~1990.
- **Gulf Coast (relative, including subsidence):** 6-9 mm/yr at stations like Grand Isle, LA and Galveston, TX.

See `reference/sea-level-rise.md` for SSP scenario details, regional projections, and observation networks.

## Compound Flooding

Compound flooding occurs when multiple flood drivers coincide or interact, producing water levels and inundation that exceed what any single driver would cause alone. This is an active research frontier.

**Driver combinations:**
- Surge + astronomical tide (well understood, routinely combined)
- Surge + wave setup + wave runup (partially coupled in ADCIRC+SWAN)
- Surge + precipitation (poorly coupled in operational systems)
- Surge + river discharge (poorly coupled -- requires hydrologic + hydrodynamic model linkage)
- Surge + sea level rise (straightforward additive for planning, but nonlinear effects in shallow estuaries)
- Precipitation + river + tide (non-TC compound flooding, increasingly studied)

**Key challenge:** No operational system jointly models all drivers. SLOSH has no rainfall. ADCIRC has no rainfall-runoff. Coupling ADCIRC with rainfall-runoff models (e.g., HEC-RAS, NWM) is an active research area. SCHISM's STOFS system is the closest to operational compound flood capability.

**Canonical case -- Hurricane Harvey (2017):** Harvey's storm surge was moderate (~3 m at Rockport, TX), but the compound effect of unprecedented rainfall (>1500 mm in 5 days over Houston) combined with coastal backwater created catastrophic flooding. Single-mechanism analysis (surge alone or rain alone) would have underestimated flood extent by 30-50%.

See `reference/compound-flooding.md` for interaction physics, joint probability methods, and case studies.

## Coastal Elevation Data and Bathymetry

### Topographic DEMs
- **CoNED (Coastal National Elevation Database):** USGS. Integrated topobathymetric DEMs covering U.S. coastline. 1/3 arc-second (~10 m) resolution. Merges lidar topography with bathymetric soundings. The standard for U.S. coastal flood modeling.
- **Lidar-derived DEMs:** 1-3 m resolution where available. USGS 3DEP (3D Elevation Program) and NOAA Digital Coast provide coverage. Essential for high-resolution inundation mapping. Vertical accuracy ~0.1-0.15 m (RMSE) on hard surfaces.
- **SRTM / Copernicus DEM:** Global coverage but insufficient vertical accuracy (1-3 m RMSE) for coastal flood studies. Useful only for reconnaissance-level assessment in data-poor regions.

### Bathymetric Data
- **GEBCO (General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans):** Global, 15 arc-second (~450 m). Adequate for deep ocean but too coarse for nearshore surge modeling.
- **NOAA Coastal Relief Model:** 3 arc-second (~90 m) for U.S. coastal waters. Better for surge model boundary conditions.
- **Multibeam surveys:** Highest quality but spatially sparse. NOAA NCEI archives survey data.

### Vertical Datums
Datum mismatches are one of the most common errors in coastal flood analysis:
- **NAVD88:** National geodetic vertical datum. Standard for USGS DEMs and most engineering.
- **MSL (Mean Sea Level):** Tidal datum. Typically 0.1-0.3 m different from NAVD88 depending on location.
- **MHHW (Mean Higher High Water):** High tide datum. Used for "above high tide" flood projections.
- **MLLW (Mean Lower Low Water):** Chart datum for NOAA nautical charts and bathymetry.
- **VDATUM:** NOAA tool for converting between 36+ tidal, orthometric, and ellipsoidal datums. Essential for merging topo and bathy data.

See `reference/coastal-data.md` for data sources, access, resolution details, and datum conversion guidance.

## FEMA Flood Mapping

- **Flood Insurance Rate Maps (FIRMs):** Regulatory maps defining Special Flood Hazard Areas (SFHAs). Determine flood insurance requirements. Zones: VE (coastal high hazard with wave action), AE (1% annual chance flood), X (minimal risk).
- **Coastal flood studies use ADCIRC+SWAN** for the 1% annual chance (100-year) and 0.2% annual chance (500-year) stillwater elevations, plus wave runup analysis.
- **Base Flood Elevation (BFE):** The computed elevation of the 1% annual chance flood including wave effects. The regulatory benchmark for building codes in SFHAs.
- **Risk Rating 2.0:** FEMA's updated flood insurance pricing methodology (effective 2021-2023). Uses property-level flood risk assessment rather than binary zone membership. Incorporates distance to coast, elevation, flood frequency, and historical claims.
- **Known limitations:** Many FIRMs are outdated (median age ~10-15 years in some regions). Do not incorporate sea level rise projections or compound flooding. Updates are slow and expensive.

## Historical Surge Events (Reference Points)

| Event | Year | Peak Surge | Key Lesson |
|-------|------|-----------|------------|
| Hurricane Katrina (MS) | 2005 | 8.5 m (28 ft) at Pass Christian | Largest surge in U.S. history. Demonstrated catastrophic failure when levees are overtopped. |
| Hurricane Sandy (NJ/NY) | 2012 | 2.7 m (9 ft) at The Battery, NYC | Moderate surge but extraordinary damage due to dense urbanization. Arrived at high tide, amplifying impact by ~0.5 m. |
| Hurricane Harvey (TX) | 2017 | ~3 m at Rockport | Moderate surge but record rainfall created compound flooding disaster in Houston. |
| Hurricane Ike (TX) | 2008 | 5.3 m (17.5 ft) at Chambers County | Large storm (R34 > 200 nmi) produced massive surge despite Cat 2 intensity at landfall. |
| Hurricane Ian (FL) | 2022 | 4.6 m (15 ft) at Fort Myers Beach | Rapid intensification before landfall. Surge exceeded SLOSH MOMs for a Cat 4 at that location. |
| Indian Ocean Tsunami | 2004 | >15 m locally | Not wind-driven; seismic. Different physics but same inundation modeling requirements. |

## AI / ML in Coastal Flooding (Emerging)

- **ADCIRC surrogates:** Neural network emulators trained on ADCIRC output to produce real-time surge predictions without running the full model. Active research at multiple institutions (Notre Dame, UNC, UT Austin). Published results show good skill for interpolating within training scenarios but limited extrapolation to unprecedented events.
- **Graph neural networks for surge:** GNNs on unstructured meshes to emulate ADCIRC solutions. Promising for rapid ensemble generation.
- **ML for compound flood prediction:** Random forests and deep learning for joint probability estimation of surge + rainfall. Early stage.
- **Flood extent from satellite imagery:** Convolutional neural networks for mapping flood extent from SAR (Sentinel-1) and optical imagery. Useful for rapid damage assessment, not forecasting.
- **Status as of March 2026:** No AI/ML surge model is operationally deployed for warnings or regulatory mapping. All remain research tools. The gap between research demonstrations and operational trust is significant.

## Key Metrics

| Metric | What it measures | Typical values |
|--------|-----------------|----------------|
| Peak surge (m) | Maximum water level above normal tide | U.S. historical range: 1-8.5 m |
| Total water level (m) | Surge + tide + wave setup + SLR | Compound metric, varies by event |
| BFE (m, NAVD88) | 1% annual chance flood elevation | U.S. coastal: 2-6 m NAVD88 typical |
| SLR rate (mm/yr) | Relative sea level change | Global: 3.6 mm/yr; Gulf Coast: 6-9 mm/yr |
| Inundation depth (m) | Water depth on land | Event-specific; drives damage functions |
| Recurrence interval (years) | Average time between exceedances | 100-year, 500-year standard for planning |
